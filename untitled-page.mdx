---
title: "Protocol revenue"
description: "The three revenue streams on Lumera, who earns from each, and how fees are distributed and burned."
---

Lumera generates revenue from three streams. Two are structural: they exist because the chain produces blocks and processes transactions. The third scales with demand for network services, and it is the one that grows as the network is actually used.

| Stream | Source | Who earns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Block rewards | Newly issued LUME | Validators and delegators |
| Transaction fees | Gas on every transaction | Validators and delegators, with a share burned |
| Service fees | Payment for executing Actions | SuperNodes |

## Block rewards

Newly issued LUME is distributed each block to validators and their delegators. Issuance is dynamic rather than fixed: it rises when the staked share of supply falls below the target bonded ratio and falls when staking exceeds it. See [supply mechanics](/token).

| Allocation | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Validators and delegators | 98% |
| Community pool | 2% |

The community pool funds protocol development, governance initiatives, and ecosystem work, and is spendable by governance proposal.

## Transaction fees

Every transaction pays a gas fee. Fees deter spam, price block space, and compensate the validators who process transactions.

| Allocation | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Validators and delegators | 80% |
| Burned | 20% |

The burn is the network's deflationary link to usage: the more the chain is used, the more LUME is permanently destroyed. This is not a fixed emission schedule running independently of activity. It responds to it.

## Service fees

Service fees are the protocol's demand-driven revenue. They are paid by whoever requests work and earned by the SuperNodes that perform it.

**Who pays.** Users, developers, and applications that execute Actions. Fees are quoted at registration, escrowed on chain, and released only when the work is verifiably complete. A failed or expired Action refunds the escrow.

**Who earns.** SuperNodes receive the full service fee for the Actions they serve. This is separate from staking: delegators do not receive service fees earned by a validator's SuperNode. Operating a SuperNode is a distinct business with distinct revenue, which is why capacity scales with demand rather than with token price alone.

**How fees are calculated.** Pricing follows the resource consumed.

| Action | Priced by |
| --- | --- |
| **Cascade** - permanent storage | Size of the data stored |
| **Sense** - content attestation | Size of the data and the analysis performed |

Cascade uses a base fee plus a per-size component. Current parameters are queryable on chain and adjustable by governance; see [how Cascade works](/cascade/how-cascade-works) for live figures.

### Keeping fees competitive

Two adjustments keep service pricing viable for both sides of the market over time.

A **dynamic adjustment factor** scales service fees against the value of LUME. Storage priced in an appreciating token would otherwise become progressively more expensive in real terms; this adjustment keeps prices competitive with alternatives rather than letting them drift with the token.

A **base annual adjustment** accounts for operators' real-world costs, such as hardware, bandwidth, and power, which do not fall just because a token rises. Together the two mechanisms keep services affordable for users while keeping operation profitable enough to retain quality operators.

## Funding permanence

Storage creates an obligation that outlives the payment for it. A single upfront fee has to cover retention indefinitely, which only works if there is a mechanism paying operators for as long as they hold data.

**Everlight** is that mechanism. It is funded from protocol reward flows, a share of registration fees, and staked endowments whose yield underwrites long-term retention. Operators are compensated continuously for data they retain, and SuperNodes must prove they still hold it to remain eligible. Permanence is therefore an economic property of the network rather than a promise from a company. See [Everlight](/cascade/everlight/overview).

## How the streams interact

The three streams are designed to offset each other rather than compete.

Block rewards secure the chain but dilute holders. Transaction fees partly compensate validators and partly destroy supply, offsetting that dilution in proportion to real usage. Service fees flow to the operators who supply capacity, so growth in demand funds growth in the infrastructure that serves it, without requiring higher issuance.

The result is a loop in which usage drives fees, fees fund capacity and remove supply, and capacity supports more usage. Revenue growth comes from more data stored, more content attested, and more work executed, rather than from raising the rate at which new tokens are issued.
